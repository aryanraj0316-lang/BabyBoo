# BabyBoo Onboarding: Screen 3 Reaction Guide by Age Group

Buddy’s reactions and factual speech-bubble lines for the question “How long does \[name\] usually get the phone in a day?”

Prepared 20 October 2026. Draft: have a pediatrician review it before launch.

## 1. Read this first

Screen 3 shows five time chips. When the parent taps one, Buddy reacts and a speech bubble shows one factual line. This guide gives the exact reaction and line for every age group and every chip, with the source for each number. Nothing is mixed between age groups: each group uses its own benchmark.

Three decisions need your confirmation:

1. **Change the age chips on Screen 2** from Under 2 · 2–3 · 4–5 · 6–7 · 8–10 to **Under 1 · 1 year · 2–4 · 5–7 · 8–10**. The guidelines change at ages 1, 2 and 5. “Under 2” mixes a baby (WHO: no screens) with a one-year-old (covered by a large 2023 study), and “4–5” straddles the WHO’s under-5 limit (1 hour) and the guideline for ages 5 and up (2 hours). The new chips keep the same count of five.
2. **Ages 5–7 and 8–10 share one table**, because the same guideline applies to both.
3. **The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) no longer sets an hour limit.** Its 2026 policy statement dropped fixed time limits, so this guide uses the WHO for under-5s and the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for ages 5 and up. Never write “the AAP limit is 1 hour” in the app.

## 2. Which benchmark applies to which age

| Group | Benchmark used | Source |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Under 1 year | No screen time recommended | WHO, 2019. AAP, 2016: avoid screens before 18 months except video chat |
| 1 year (12–23 months) | Sedentary screen time not recommended | WHO, 2019. Developmental findings from Takahashi et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2023 |
| 2–4 years | No more than 1 hour a day of sedentary screen time; less is better | WHO, 2019 |
| 5–10 years | No more than 2 hours a day of recreational screen time (not schoolwork); 9 to 11 hours of sleep for ages 5–13 | Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for Children and Youth, 2016 |

Not used: the AAP’s older “1 hour for ages 2–5” and “2 hours” limits. They were replaced in January 2026 by a framework that asks what screens are replacing (sleep, play, reading, family time) instead of counting minutes. It can still be mentioned in a “learn more” card for older children, as shown in section 6.

## 3. Buddy’s five reaction levels

| Level | Expression | When it is used |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| L1 | Happy, small thumbs-up | At or under the benchmark |
| L2 | Smiling, nodding | At the limit, or a small gentle note |
| L3 | Curious, head tilt, one raised eyebrow | Over the benchmark by a noticeable amount |
| L4 | Worried, small gasp, wide eyes | Well over the benchmark |
| L5 | Hand over his mouth, eyes very wide, but still warm | Far over the benchmark, only in the groups where it applies |

Rules for the reactions:

- L5 is always paired with “Let’s fix this together” and is never used for ages 5–10, where the guideline is 2 hours and the highest chip is only about twice that.
- Buddy never frowns at the parent, never uses the words “too much”, “bad” or “dangerous”, and never looks angry. His worst expression is worry.
- The reaction plays when the chip is tapped and settles back to a calm smile after about two seconds, so the parent isn’t left looking at a distressed mascot.

## 4. Wording rules for every bubble

- **Fact first, then partnership.** Higher chips end with a line like “Let’s make a plan together.”
- **Say “linked to” or “higher odds”.** Never “causes”, “damages”, “harms the brain” or “ruins”. Most of this research shows associations, and the average effects are small.
- **One fact per bubble**, 25 words or fewer.
- **Show the source** in small gray text under the bubble, for example “Source: WHO, 2019”.
- **Study lines get one more small line:** “Patterns across many children, not a prediction for \[name\].” This matters for parents whose child already has delays, who should not be made to feel blamed.
- **Use the child’s name** where it reads naturally.
- **Use only the numbers in this document.** Do not add other statistics without checking them.
- **No parent-blaming.** The AAP’s 2026 statement itself aims to take shame off parents, and the app should do the same.

## 5. The question wording

| Group | Question text |
| :-- | :-- |
| Under 1, 1 year, 2–4 | “How long does \[name\] usually get the phone in a day?” |
| 5–7 and 8–10 | “How long does \[name\] usually get the phone in a day, not counting schoolwork?” |

The second version matters because the 2-hour guideline covers recreational screen time only.

### Group B: 1 year (12–23 months)

Chips: Almost none · Under 1 hour · 1 to 2 hours · 2 to 4 hours · More than 4 hours. These match the categories used in the 2023 study (under 1 hour, 1 to under 2, 2 to under 4, 4 or more).

| Chip | Buddy | Bubble text | Source |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Almost none | L1 | “That’s in line with the WHO, which advises against screen time at age 1.” | WHO, 2019 |
| Under 1 hour | L2 | “The WHO advises against screens at age 1. In a big 2023 study, under 1 hour a day was the group with the fewest delays.” | WHO, 2019; Takahashi et al., 2023 |
| 1 to 2 hours | L3 | “A big 2023 study linked 1 to 2 hours a day at age 1 to higher odds of language delay by age 2.” | Takahashi et al., 2023 (odds ratio 1.61) |
| 2 to 4 hours | L4 | “In a 2023 study of 7,097 children, 2 to 4 hours a day at age 1 went with about double the odds of a language delay at age 2. Let’s work on it together.” | Takahashi et al., 2023 (odds ratio 2.04) |
| More than 4 hours | L5 | “In a 2023 study, language delays at age 2 were 14.8% with 4+ hours a day at age 1, against 3.5% with under 1 hour. Let’s fix this together.” | Takahashi et al., 2023 |

For the three study lines, add the small print: “Patterns across many children, not a prediction for \[name\].”

“Learn more” card for this group: *In this Japanese study of 7,097 mother-child pairs, parents reported screen time at age 1, and development was screened at ages 2 and 4. More screen time went with more delays in communication and problem-solving, in a step-by-step pattern. The data come from parent reports, so they show a link, not proof of cause.*

| Source | Status |

| WHO, 2019, guidelines for children under 5 | Rules confirmed across several reports of the guideline; WHO’s own publication page was located, but the full text was not read |
| AAP, 2016, “Media and Young Minds” | Confirmed through secondary reports (no screens before 18 months except video chat; 1 hour for ages 2–5 in that edition) |

| Takahashi et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2023 (7,097 children) | Odds ratios confirmed from the published abstract; the 14.8% and 3.5% figures come from a pediatrician’s summary of the paper and should be checked against the full text |

## 9. Fixes needed in other BabyBoo materials

- **The “3x” figure from the earlier science-screen draft is wrong.** It came from a news summary saying 1-year-olds with “up to four hours” had three times the delays. The study’s own numbers, against under 1 hour a day, are about 1.6x (1 to 2 hours), about 2x (2 to 4 hours) and about 4.8x (4 or more hours) the odds of a communication delay at age 2. Use the lines in Group B.
- **The product spec’s WHO row** (“under 5: 60 minutes”) should be replaced with the age-specific rules in section 2.
- **The spec’s AAP 2016 row** should note that the AAP removed fixed hour limits in January 2026.

## 10. Extra language-delay facts for the “Learn more” cards

These add stronger, still-accurate language facts on top of the bubble lines above. Each is a tap-to-open card, shown after the bubble for the chips listed. The bubble stays one short fact; the card carries the detail.

| Card | Shown for | Text | Source |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| L1. Delay rates at age 2 | Group B (1 year), chips 2 to 4 hours and more than 4 hours | “Language (communication) delays at age 2 were 3.5% with under 1 hour a day at age 1, and 14.8% with 4+ hours. Problem-solving delays were 3.4% and 10%.” | Takahashi et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2023 (7,097 children); percentages from a pediatrician’s summary, to be checked against the full paper |
| L2. It lasts | Group B, chips 2 to 4 hours and more than 4 hours | “The link was still there at age 4: communication delays were 8.6% with 4+ hours a day at age 1, against 4.0% with under 1 hour.” | Takahashi et al., 2023 |
| L3. The words a child doesn’t hear | Group C (2–4), chip About 3 hours | “In an Australian study, each extra minute of screen time at age 3 went with 6.6 fewer adult words spoken to the child, 4.9 fewer child sounds and words, and 1.1 fewer back-and-forth turns. At the study’s average of 172 minutes a day (about 3 hours), the researchers estimated a child could miss about 1,100 adult words and about 190 conversational turns every day.” | Screen Time and Parent-Child Talk When Children Are Aged 12 to 36 Months, JAMA Pediatrics, 2024 |
| L4. Why talk matters | Group C, chips About 2 hours and above | “When a television was audible, adults spoke roughly 500 to 1,000 fewer words per hour to young children, and children made fewer sounds and had fewer conversations.” | Christakis et al., Pediatrics, 2009 |
| L5. The one-minute rule | Group C, all chips from About 2 hours | “Every minute of screen time is a minute that isn’t spent talking with Leo.” | This is plain wording of the 2024 finding above, not a new statistic |

Rules for these cards:

- Open every card with its source line and end it with “Patterns across many children, not a prediction for \[name\].”
- Do not do your own arithmetic on these figures. The “1,100 words” and “190 turns” come from the researchers’ own estimate at the study’s average screen time, so the card is tied to the About 3 hours chip only.
- The Australian study followed children from 12 to 36 months and measured the strongest effect at 36 months, so use wording “at age 3” and show it only to the 2–4 group.

### The honest limits (keep these in the small print)

A 2026 review of this research notes that most of the evidence linking screen time to early language is correlational, that effects are generally small, and that they shrink when other family factors are taken into account. The same review found that high screen time combined with little back-and-forth talk was linked to poorer language outcomes, and that watching together and talking while watching is what the study authors suggest. This is why every card says “linked to” and why Buddy’s closing line is always about a plan, not blame.

### What the app must not say

- “What Leo sees on the phone stays with him forever”, “permanent”, “irreversible”, or “damaged”. Early experience matters and predicts later outcomes, but learning continues throughout childhood and nothing in this research says early exposure cannot be outgrown.
- Any number for how much BabyBoo raises intelligence or improves behavior. BabyBoo has no outcome data yet. Use the time-saved arithmetic and the research above, and add real parent-reported results after the beta.
- That BabyBoo blocks bad words or violent content. It limits time, not content, and the app says so on Screen 6.
