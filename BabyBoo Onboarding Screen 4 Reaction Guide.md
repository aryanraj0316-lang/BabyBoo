# BabyBoo Onboarding: Screen 4 Reaction Guide (When Screen Time Ends)

Buddy’s reactions and bubble lines for the question “What usually happens when \[name\]’s screen time ends?”, by age group, with the facts behind each line.

Prepared 24 October 2026. Draft: have a pediatrician review it before launch.

## 1. Read this first

Screen 3 compared the child’s screen time with a guideline, so Buddy’s reactions rose from happy to alarmed. **Screen 4 is different.** It asks about the parent’s hardest moment, so Buddy only shows **empathy**, never alarm. No chip gets a worried or alarmed face.

Facts appear here for only three jobs:

1. **To normalize.** Tantrums are common, which tells the parent they are not failing.
2. **To say when to ask a doctor,** in one gentle line, with no numbers or diagnoses.
3. **One honest concern:** using a phone to calm an upset young child has been linked to more emotional outbursts later. It appears only on the “I give in” chip for ages 2–4.

The age groups are the same as in the Screen 3 guide: Under 1, 1 year, 2–4, and 5–10 (ages 5–7 and 8–10 share one table).

## 2. Buddy’s empathy poses

| Pose | Expression | Used for |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| E1 | Happy, small thumbs-up | Smooth endings |
| E2 | Smiling, nodding | Mild whining or grumbling |
| E3 | Sympathetic, hand on heart | Crying, tantrums, arguing |
| E4 | Gentle and concerned, offers a hug | Full meltdowns |
| E5 | Warm, knowing smile | “I give in” |

Rules for every pose and line:

- Buddy never frowns at the parent, never looks alarmed, and never implies the parent is at fault.
- Never say a child “has” a condition. Never use words like disorder, ADHD or problem.
- “Mention it to your pediatrician” appears only in the lines below, and always gently.
- For ages 5–10, say arguing, shouting or grumbling. The word tantrum feels babyish to those parents.
- One fact per bubble, 25 words or fewer, with the source in small gray text under it. Study lines also get: “Patterns across many children, not a prediction for \[name\].”
- No time thresholds (such as “15 minutes”) appear on screen, because sources disagree on them (15, 20 and 25 minutes all appear).

## 3. The facts used

| Fact | Source |
| :-- | :-- |
| Tantrums happen in 87% of children aged 18–24 months, 91% aged 30–36 months, and 59% aged 42–48 months | Temper Tantrums, StatPearls (NCBI) review, which reports the underlying studies |
| The median tantrum lasts about 3 minutes, and about 75% last 5 minutes or less | Same |
| Daily tantrums occur in about 20% of 2-year-olds, 18% of 3-year-olds, and 10% of 4-year-olds | Same |
| Tantrums are much less common at school age, and recurrent or severe ones are a reason to seek help | AACAP, Facts for Families No. 136, June 2025; StatPearls (a repeated pattern after age 5 is atypical) |
| In 422 children aged 3 to 5 followed for six months, frequently using phones or tablets to calm upset children was linked to more emotional dysregulation (quick mood shifts, impulsivity), especially in boys and in children who already react strongly | Radesky et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2022 |
| The lead researcher said the relief is real in the short term, but a device distracts instead of teaching a skill, and the habit can strengthen over time. She suggested timers and clear boundaries, and talking through feelings when the child is calm | Press coverage of the same study (expert advice, not a finding of the study itself) |

## 5. Optional “Learn more” cards

Shown as a small tap-to-open link under the bubble, for the chips listed. Each ends with its source.

| Card | Shown for | Text |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| T1. How common are tantrums? | Groups B and C, chips Crying or a tantrum and A full meltdown | “Tantrums are a normal part of growing up. A review of studies found them in 87% of children aged 18 to 24 months, 91% at 30 to 36 months and 59% at 42 to 48 months. Most last about 3 minutes, and three in four last 5 minutes or less. Daily tantrums happen in about 20% of 2-year-olds, 18% of 3-year-olds and 10% of 4-year-olds.” Source: StatPearls review. |
| T2. When to ask for help | Groups B, C and D, chip A full meltdown | “Most tantrums are a normal part of growing up. A pediatrician can help if tantrums are very frequent or very long, or if a child hurts themselves or others. Tantrums that keep happening often after age 5 are worth mentioning too.” Source: AACAP, 2025; StatPearls. |
| T3. Using a phone to calm | Group C, chip I give in | “In a study of 422 children aged 3 to 5, followed for six months, parents who often used phones or tablets to calm upset children saw more quick mood shifts and impulsive behavior over time, especially in boys and in children who already react strongly. The lead researcher said the relief is real in the short term, but a device distracts instead of teaching a skill, and the habit can grow stronger. She suggested timers, clear limits, and talking through feelings when the child is calm.” Source: Radesky et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2022. |

Limits to keep in view for T3: it is based on parent reports and shows a link, not proof. Giving in to extend screen time is closely related to using a device to calm a child, but not identical, which is why the bubble says “often using phones to calm upset kids” and not “giving in”.

## 6. What happens to the answer

- The answer is saved as the handover type: smooth, mild, tantrum, meltdown or give\_in.
- The “rewrite Leo’s day” screen uses it: crying or meltdown fills the red line “It ends with crying or a tantrum”, and “I give in” fills “‘Just 5 more minutes.’ Again.”
- A parent who picks “Goes smoothly” sees fewer pain rows on that screen, as agreed.
- No answer on this screen ever produces a diagnosis or a warning. At most it produces the soft “mention it to your pediatrician” line.
