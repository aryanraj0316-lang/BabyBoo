# BabyBoo Onboarding: Screen 6 Reaction Guide (Content the Child Sees)

Buddy’s reactions and bubble lines for the multi-select question “What does \[name\] mostly pick up from the phone?”, by age group, with the facts behind each line.

Prepared 24 October 2026. Draft: have a pediatrician review it before launch.

## 1. Read this first

- **The question is multi-select** (“Pick all that apply”). Buddy reacts to each tap, and the bubble shows the line for the most recent tap. Choosing “Nothing worrying so far” clears every other option.
- **One standing line appears once** after any worrying option is chosen, in small gray text: “I can’t choose what \[name\] watches, but I can limit how long. Less time means less exposure.” BabyBoo limits time, not content, and the app must never imply that it blocks or filters anything.
- **Facts here are about content on screens in general,** not about BabyBoo. None of them is a promise about what BabyBoo will change.
- **A new option for you to approve:** “Ads and toy videos” (shown as “Ads and in-app spending” for ages 5–10). I added it because the data is strong. If you decline it, delete those rows and the C3 card.
- **Age groups** are the same as in the Screen 3 and 4 guides: Under 1, 1 year, 2–4, and 5–10. The rude-words option is left out for under 1, because babies do not speak.

## 2. Buddy’s reactions on this screen

| Option type | Pose | Description |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Songs, rhymes, learning, creating | L1 | Happy |
| Nothing worrying so far | L1 | Happy with a small thumbs-up |
| Rude words or slang; ads | L3 | Curious, head tilt, one raised eyebrow |
| Hitting, fights, violent content | L4 (ages 1–4) or L3 (ages 5–10) | Worried with a small gasp for the young groups. The evidence is strongest for ages 1–3, so ages 5–10 get the milder pose |
| Scary or grown-up scenes | E4 | Gentle and concerned, offers a hug |

Rules for every line: one fact per bubble, 25 words or fewer, with the source in small gray text under it. Say “links” or “linked to”, never “causes”. Never say the child has any condition. Never blame the parent. Study lines also get the small print “Patterns across many children, not a prediction for \[name\].” Lines based on small studies add “Small study.”

## 3. The facts used

| Fact | Source |
| :-- | :-- |
| In 426 YouTube videos watched by 47 children under 3, only 19% were made for their age (81% were not), 27% showed physical violence, 15% had scary content, 48% promoted products, and only 6% had a learning goal | Henderson et al., Frontiers in Developmental Psychology, 2024 (main YouTube app, data from 2020, small sample) |
| Babies aged 0–11 months watched more violent and age-inappropriate videos than children aged 24–35 months | Same |
| Music videos had much less violence (8% against 34%) and product promotion (11% against 62%) than other videos. Informational videos had no physical violence (0% against 29%). Gaming videos stood out: 64% showed physical violence and 89% promoted products | Same |
| Children as young as 14 months can imitate behavior from a screen, and the authors see ages 1–3 as a period of heightened sensitivity to violent content | Same (their discussion, citing earlier studies) |
| Violent TV at ages 1–3 was linked to more attention problems five years later, but not at ages 4–5. In a randomized trial, replacing violent TV with age-appropriate programs reduced behavior problems in preschool boys | Zimmerman and Christakis, Pediatrics, 2007; Christakis et al., Pediatrics, 2013 (both as summarized by Henderson et al., 2024) |
| A review by the American Academy of Pediatrics found a significant link between media violence and aggressive behavior, aggressive thoughts and angry feelings | AAP policy statement on virtual violence |
| Young children imitate what they see, even fantasy characters | JAMA Pediatrics review of media violence research |
| 61% of parents said their child had come across unsuitable content on YouTube | Pew Research Center, 2018 (as cited by Henderson et al., 2024) |
| 95% of 135 popular apps for children 5 and under contained at least one type of advertising (100% of free apps, 88% of paid apps), and in-app purchases appeared in about a third. Children under 8 have difficulty recognizing advertising | Meyer et al., Journal of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics, 2019; APA task force on advertising and children, 2004 (as cited) |

## 6. After onboarding: a tip card for the app

The standing line says BabyBoo limits how long, not what, so give parents something useful about what. The authors of the 2024 YouTube study suggest that caregivers of young children pre-select videos, choosing music or informational genres and avoiding gaming and compilation videos. A short tip card on the Home screen, shown once after onboarding, can say exactly that, with the source. Never present it as a BabyBoo feature.

## 7. What the app must not say

- That BabyBoo blocks, filters or hides any content, including bad words and violence.
- That a type of content causes a child to behave a certain way. Use “linked to”.
- Any statistic about slang or swearing. None was found.
- Any percentage from a study for an age group it did not study (for example, the 81% figure for a 7-year-old).
